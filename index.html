Research note, August 2026

Who else is doing this, and are they doing it to us.

We are past 8,850 homes and aiming at 50,000 by 2030. That puts us in the same conversation as Grainger, Legal and General and M&G. The useful thing is that we are not really doing the same job they are, and I think that difference is worth understanding before anyone reads across their numbers to ours.

2.2bn

Savills, second quarter 2026

Pounds put into UK build to rent in one quarter, the strongest second quarter on record.

Knight Frank counts 26,407 operating single family rental homes in the UK, about 16 percent of all build to rent stock. So a record amount of money is arriving into a corner of the market that is still small. That is good for the value of what we already own and harder for what we want to buy next.

The short version

Grainger owns more homes than any other listed landlord in the UK and almost all of them are flats in city centres. Legal and General is spread across several housing tenures at once. M&G invests in the sector rather than running homes day to day. We are the one building a large book of family houses in suburbs and market towns, managed by our own team. Four businesses in the same industry doing four different jobs.

If you remember one thing

Our closest match on strategy is the suburban arm of Legal and General. Our closest match on size is Grainger. Nobody is doing both at our speed.

How the four of us compare on size

Hover a bar for the detail. The pale bar is where we have said we want to be by 2030, and it is worth seeing it next to where everyone is today.

Grainger

A FTSE 250 business, founded in Newcastle in 1912, and a real estate investment trust since 2025. It reports over 11,200 rental homes and a secured pipeline of just under 4,000 more worth around 1.2 billion pounds. The stock is mostly purpose built blocks in Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds and London, with on site teams and shared lounges and gyms.

What that means for us is simple. We compete with Grainger for money and for good operations people. We almost never compete for the same tenant. A family looking for a three bed house in Hucknall is not weighing that up against a studio in central Manchester.

Legal and General

Legal and General has put more than 3 billion pounds into build to rent and reports over 10,000 rental homes across its residential platform. Its suburban arm started at the end of 2020 and does the closest thing to what we do, buying family houses from housebuilders such as Miller Homes and letting them as whole communities. It has also raised debt specifically against a suburban pipeline of around 1,000 homes.

This is the one to watch. When a housebuilder has a portfolio of finished houses to sell, we are usually bidding against them. Our advantage is the depth of the Barratt Redrow relationship, now over 3,000 homes across three separate deals, which makes us an easy company to come back to.

M&G Real Estate

M&G is an investor first. It buys into build to rent schemes and, in research published this year, argues that suburban family rental performs better than blocks of flats because the homes sit empty less often and cost less to keep up over time. That is the same argument our business case rests on, which is handy.

Useful for us

When someone challenges the yield on family houses against flats, we can point at published M&G research rather than only at our own model.

The rules changed too

The Renters Rights Act removes no fault evictions from May 2026, moves tenancies to open ended agreements and limits rent increases to once a year. For a landlord that already plans on long tenancies and one review a year, this is mainly a cost of process rather than a hit to income. It does raise the bar on how well you have to run the operation, which quietly favours the bigger professional owners.

If you remember one thing

A record quarter of investment plus a small single family base means more buyers chasing the same housebuilder stock. Our contracted pipeline out to 2029 is worth more now than it looked on the day it was signed.

What we have bought

One, two, three and four bed houses across 14 developments in Nottinghamshire, Merseyside, East Anglia, the West Midlands, Cambridgeshire and Northamptonshire. They reach us between autumn 2026 and the end of 2029. Every home is EPC B or better, many have air source heat pumps, and our own team manages all of them.

How I have set the rent

I start with what the same size of house lets for locally, second hand. Then I add a small amount for a new build with low running costs, a private garden and a landlord who answers the phone. I hold us below the top of the local range on purpose, because letting quickly is worth more than the last twenty pounds a month.

The numbers behind everything else

Five sheets. The asset register holds the stock, the rent sheet holds the price, the budget sheet turns those two into a yield, and the last two show what happens when the world does not behave. Nothing is typed in. Every figure is calculated, and you can see the working with the formulas button.

The two things I get asked about most are voids and rent growth, so I have put them on sliders. Move them and watch the whole model and the committee pack move with you.

Asset register

Every site, the capital it takes and the rent it should make once it is full. Click a column heading to sort. Cost per home is a modelled regional average.

Formulas on this sheet

  • Homes on a site= 1bed + 2bed + 3bed + 4bed
  • Capital= Homes × Cost per home
  • Gross rent a year= SUM(units by bed × monthly rent by bed) × 12
  • Gross yield on cost= Gross rent a year / Capital
  • Portfolio cost per home= SUM(Capital) / SUM(Homes)

Gross rent here assumes every home is let. Nothing is taken off for empty homes or missed payments yet, that happens on the budget sheet.

Rent assumptions by region

Monthly asking rents, set from local evidence for the same size of house with a small premium for a new build in our management. The slider above applies growth to all of them at once.

Formulas on this sheet

  • Homes in a region= SUMIF(Region, this region, Homes)
  • Gross rent a year= SUMIF(Region, this region, Site gross rent)
  • Weighted average rent= Gross rent a year / Homes / 12
  • Rent with growth applied= Base rent × (1 + growth)

Rents get reviewed once a year, which is what the Renters Rights Act allows. Merseyside is the cheapest region in the book and Cambridgeshire the dearest, which is roughly a three hundred pound a month gap on a three bed.

Budget and stabilised yield

The whole portfolio once it is full and settled, so 2030 in practice. Costs are annual, and shown per home as well because that is how we actually control them.

Formulas on this sheet

  • Void allowance= Gross rent × void rate
  • Bad debt= Gross rent × 0.8%
  • Net rent collected= Gross rent × (1 − void rate − 0.8%)
  • Management cost= Gross rent × 3.5%
  • Per home costs= Homes × (550 + 185 + 140 + 95 + 75)
  • Net operating income= Net rent collected − Management − Per home costs
  • Total capital= Purchase price × (1 + 1.8%)
  • Gross yield= Gross rent / Total capital
  • Net stabilised yield= Net operating income / Total capital

Management is the internal recharge for our own team. There is no letting agent fee in here because we do not use one, which is worth about half a million pounds a year against an outsourced model.

Occupancy and arrears

Twelve months on the 598 homes we bought from Barratt Redrow in 2025. It is the closest thing we have to a preview of how the new portfolio behaves once it lets up. Hover the chart for any month.

Formulas on this sheet

  • Occupancy= Homes let / 598
  • Rent due= Homes let × Average rent
  • Rent collected= Rent due × Collection rate
  • Arrears= Rent due × Arrears share
  • Twelve month collection= SUM(Rent collected) / SUM(Rent due)

It took about nine months to get from handover to 97 percent let, and collection improved as the estates settled. That is the shape I have assumed for every new site in the pipeline, which is why the mobilisation spend is front loaded.

Sensitivity

Net stabilised yield as voids and rent growth move. Click any cell to set the sliders to it and take the rest of the model with you. The outlined cell is wherever the sliders are now.

Formulas on this sheet

  • Grown gross rent= Gross rent × (1 + rent growth)
  • Income losses= Grown gross rent × (void rate + 0.8% + 3.5%)
  • Net operating income= Grown gross rent − Income losses − Per home costs
  • Cell value= Net operating income / Total capital

Read it as a range, not a forecast. The point I want people to take away is that voids move the answer more than rent growth does in the early years. That is the argument for spending money on mobilisation rather than hoping the market carries us.
